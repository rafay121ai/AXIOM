## Situation 12 (think_sharper, planning)

### X

**What the author argues** (Nassim Taleb, The Black Swan): Nassim Taleb in The Black Swan argues that most forecasts are unreliable because they ignore error rates, underestimate how much accuracy degrades over time, and fail to consider the full range of possible outcomes—including worst-case scenarios. He says, 'Forecasting without incorporating an error rate uncovers three fallacies, all arising from the same misconception about the nature of uncertainty.'

> Forecasting without incorporating an error rate uncovers three fallacies, all arising from the same misconception about the nature of uncertainty. The first fallacy: variability matters. The first error lies in taking a projection too seriously, without heeding its accuracy.

**How it connects to you:** Your last year's model was off by 3x, showing high variability and error. Taleb's argument applies directly: you should not take your $1M ARR forecast at face value, but instead focus on the range of possible outcomes and explicitly include error rates. This is crucial before making hiring decisions based on a single forecast.

**Do this:** Add an explicit error range (e.g., +/- 50%) to your ARR forecast and write down what hiring decisions you would make at both the low and high ends of that range.

### Y

**What the author argues** (Nassim Taleb, The Black Swan): Nassim Taleb in The Black Swan argues that the accuracy of your forecast matters far more than the forecast itself, and that you must incorporate an error rate and consider the range of possible outcomes, not just a single number. He warns that forecast errors are often enormous, especially as the time horizon lengthens, and that decisions should focus on the worst-case scenario, as it is far more consequential than the forecast itself.

> The policies we need to make decisions on should depend far more on the range of possible outcomes than on the expected final number. ... it is the lower bound of estimates (i.e., the worst case) that matters when engaging in a policy—the worst case is far more consequential than the forecast itself.

**How it connects to you:** Your last financial model was off by 3x, showing high variability and uncertainty. Taleb's argument applies directly: you should not base hiring on a single $1M ARR forecast, but instead consider the range of possible outcomes and especially the worst-case scenario, since hiring against an over-optimistic forecast could have severe consequences.

He also emphasizes writing down what you want in the role and sharing it with your team to ensure alignment. Finally, he notes that making some hiring mistakes is normal and correctable, so founders shouldn't let fear paralyze them.

> Most of the time, a founder will have no idea what to look for when hiring someone to run a particular company function. ... If you want to learn more about what a great CFO or VP of engineering does, your best bet is to reach out to people who are great at these roles and ask them for advice.

**How it connects to you:** You have no prior sales management experience and are afraid of being fooled by a great interview. Gil's advice directly addresses your situation: use your network and investors to connect with experienced heads of sales, learn what makes someone great in that role, and define what you need for your company's current stage. This reduces the risk of hiring based on interview performance alone and helps you set clear expectations for success.

**Do this:** Ask your investors or mentors today for introductions to 3-4 experienced heads of sales at companies a few years ahead of yours. Set up short calls to learn what to look for and what questions to ask when hiring your first head of sales.
